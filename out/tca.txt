					<description><![CDATA[Lawmakers in the Kurultai, Kazakhstan’s parliament, drafted legislation with AI assistance for the first time, Deputy Speaker Dinara Zakiyeva said]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Lawmakers in the Kurultai, Kazakhstan’s parliament, drafted legislation with <a href="https://prg.kz/document/?doc_id=39195207" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">AI assistance</a> for the first time, Deputy Speaker Dinara Zakiyeva said on November 16. They used the AI-Quryltai platform to prepare the bill “On Children’s Digital and Information Safety.” It would require digital platforms to offer <a href="https://timesca.com/kazakhstan-online-child-safety-bill-games-apps/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">parental controls</a> and restrict children’s access to unlabeled or unverified games and apps.</p>
<p>The platform helped lawmakers analyze public submissions and feedback from parents and experts, as well as review how other countries protect children online. Zakiyeva said its model trained for legal analysis draws on a database of more than 800,000 laws and regulations. Lawmakers used it to check proposed changes against existing rules and identify potential conflicts. They drafted more than 120 amendments while working on the bill. Zakiyeva did not specify how many were based on AI suggestions.</p>
<p>Lawmakers <a href="https://www.zakon.kz/sobytiia/6538948-vpervye-razrabotan-zakonoproekt-s-pomoshchyu-iiplatformy-AIQuryltai.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">reviewed and refined</a> every suggestion from the platform with help from outside lawyers and digital safety specialists, according to the Kurultai’s press service. Yekaterina Smyshlyayeva, who heads the Kurultai’s AI lab, said parliament also plans to use the platform to assess the likely effects of proposed rules and track how they are applied once adopted.</p>
<p>A few days before Zakiyeva’s announcement, a delegation from Kazakhstan <a href="https://www.gov.kz/org/mfa-geneva/press/news/details/1329759?lang=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">presented AI-Quryltai</a> in Geneva alongside parliament’s 2027–2029 Digital Transformation Strategy. The platform builds on e-Quryltai, parliament’s existing digital system, and is also designed to <a href="https://www.inform.kz/ru/kazahstanskuyu-ii-platformu-dlyaraboti-deputatov-predstavili-vzheneve-3f6b4dad" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">prepare briefings</a> for lawmakers and parliamentary staff. It is still in a <a href="https://informburo.kz/novosti/pervyi-zakonoproekt-s-pomoshhiu-ii-razrabotali-v-kazaxstane" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">pilot phase</a>, with the AI lab testing individual features.</p>
<p>Kazakhstan already uses AI to review applications for targeted social assistance. The system <a href="https://primeminister.kz/ru/news/ii-povyshaet-obektivnost-i-skorost-okazaniya-socialnyh-uslug-31759" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">analyzes photos and videos</a> of applicants’ homes and makes recommendations about whether a household needs assistance. On October 16, the government said it had used the technology to process <a href="https://primeminister.kz/ru/news/digital_office/olzhas-bektenov-provel-soveshchanie-po-voprosam-cifrovoy-transformacii-sfery-truda-i-socialnoy-zashchity-naseleniya-32004" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">more than 183,000 applications</a>. Final decisions on benefits still follow the standard process.</p>
<p>Separately, Kazakhstan’s Ministry of Justice <a href="https://timesca.com/kazakhstan-to-integrate-ai-into-lawmaking-process/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">nnounced plans</a> in October 2025 for an AI assistant to help draft laws and regulations. The ministry said the tool would review legal practices in other countries and flag provisions that might conflict with the Constitution.</p>

					<description><![CDATA[Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan are spending public money to attract international AI companies. They are financing infrastructure and offering preferential electricity]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan are spending public money to attract international AI companies. They are financing infrastructure and offering preferential electricity tariffs to secure a place in the global AI industry. The return should include opportunities for domestic technology businesses, as well as income from hosting foreign servers.</p>
<p>The division of costs and ownership deserves scrutiny. Foreign partners supply advanced processors and bring international customers, while domestic institutions finance the buildings and supporting infrastructure. How much of the resulting income will stay in the host economies, and what will local developers gain?</p>
<p><strong>Kazakhstan: Domestic Infrastructure, Foreign Hardware</strong></p>
<p>In Kazakhstan’s Data Center Valley in Ekibastuz, the division is explicit. In June 2026, the government announced a <a href="https://primeminister.kz/en/news/data-center-valley-kazakhstan-government-firebird-and-nvidia-sign-10-billion-package-of-agreements-in-artificial-intelligence-31516" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">$10 billion package</a> of agreements with Firebird and NVIDIA.</p>
<p>Kazakhstan’s KT-Telecom will finance the buildings and engineering infrastructure, including electrical and cooling systems. Firebird will pay for the computing equipment and retain ownership, according to the Ministry of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Development’s <a href="https://bes.media/news/za-ispolzovanie-moschnostey-nvidia-v-doline-tsodov-kazahstantsam-pridetsya-platit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">response to BES.media</a>. KT-Telecom is jointly owned by Kazakhtelecom and Samruk-Kazyna Invest, each holding a 50% stake.</p>
<p>Financial disclosures covered by BES.media show that KT-Telecom secured a <a href="https://bes.media/news/dochka-kazahtelekoma-vzyala-kredit-na-stroyku-doliny-tsodov/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">50 billion tenge loan</a> from the Development Bank of Kazakhstan in June 2026. The loan runs for 15 years at an annual interest rate of 12.6%. The company also raised $112.5 million through bonds listed on the Astana International Exchange at 6% interest, maturing in 2036. A subsequent bond issue raised approximately 594 million Chinese yuan at 4.5%.</p>
<p>KT-Telecom received a government subsidy of 13.3 billion tenge for Data Center Valley. The company has not yet recognized it as income because the subsidized facilities have not entered operation. Kazakhstan’s state-linked institutions are taking on long-term debt to build the facilities.</p>
<p>The government has approved a <a href="https://primeminister.kz/en/news/olzhas-bektenov-holds-meeting-on-implementation-of-the-data-center-valley-project-in-fulfillment-of-presidents-instructions-31285" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">preferential electricity tariff</a> model, and a 215-megawatt substation has been acquired. In its <a href="https://primeminister.kz/en/news/digital-economy-olzhas-bektenov-holds-meeting-on-the-implementation-of-the-data-center-valley-project-32091" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">November 6 project update</a>, the government outlined plans for three data centers totaling 304 megawatts, with expansion to one gigawatt envisaged by 2029. These government updates do not give a full account of who will pay for additional electricity generation and wider network upgrades.</p>
<p><strong>Uzbekistan: Public Infrastructure and Private Investment</strong></p>
<p>Uzbekistan is offering tax incentives and publicly funded infrastructure to attract computing companies. In September 2026, President Shavkat Mirziyoyev announced six AI and data center projects <a href="https://www.president.uz/en/lists/view/9642" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">worth $8 billion</a> in Karakalpakstan. He instructed officials to develop a five-year program to ensure a stable electricity supply of one gigawatt for investors.</p>
<p>Under <a href="https://lex.uz/docs/7789403" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">Presidential Decree No. PF-189</a> of October 2025, qualifying AI infrastructure projects in Karakalpakstan’s designated zone that exceed $100 million receive tax and customs benefits, alongside an electricity tariff of $0.05 per kilowatt-hour, excluding value-added tax. Uzbekistan’s official <a href="https://www.outsource.gov.uz/en/explore-opportunities/data-center" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">investment portal</a> specifies that the government will finance external infrastructure, including electricity connections and other utilities, provided eligible investors contribute at least 25% equity.</p>
<p>In return for these incentives, data center operators must install battery storage systems rated at 30–35% of their electrical load. They must also reduce electricity demand during designated peak hours, when electricity costs 1.5 times the preferential rate.</p>
<p>The government aims to create 1,500 high-paying jobs within three years and raise annual exports of cloud and AI services to $600 million. Those exports would bring in foreign revenue, but imported equipment and payments to foreign investors would absorb part of it. The domestic return also needs to justify the public infrastructure spending and tax concessions.</p>
<p><strong>What Local Businesses Gain</strong></p>
<p>Access to computing resources gives local businesses a more direct stake in these investments. In October, Firebird committed to allocating <a href="https://www.firebird.ai/news-kazakhstans-ai-ambitions-accelerate-as-firebird-secures-45-000-nvidia-grace-blac.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">at least 10%</a> of its graphics processing unit capacity in Kazakhstan to Kazakhstani companies and government agencies. It also announced plans to invest in local startups and give them access to computing resources. The announcement gives no prices or detailed access terms. Those terms will determine whether smaller developers can afford to use the reserved capacity.</p>
<p>Large domestic customers are already buying computing services. In September, Bagdat Mussin, chairman of Kazakhtelecom’s management board, said Kaspi.kz had secured <a href="https://digitalbusiness.kz/2026-09-16/70-superkompyutera-kazahstana-srazu-zabral-kaspi-kz-bagdat-musin-rasskazal-zachem/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">70% of the computing capacity</a> of Kazakhtelecom’s Al-Farabium cluster for two years.</p>
<p>Uzbekistan is supporting local developers through an <a href="https://gov.uz/en/digital/news/view/235892" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">October 2026 government resolution</a>. Eligible startups and research institutions working on socially significant AI projects can use state computing infrastructure free or at preferential rates.</p>
<p>Qualifying projects using large language models can receive free access through a software interface connecting their applications to the models for up to six months. The monthly allowance is 10 million tokens, the units of text processed by the models. Access is competitive, and commercial use of the data obtained is restricted unless permitted by law or contract. This support is separate from the incentives for foreign data center operators. It lowers development costs for selected local projects, though businesses hoping to sell products built with that support must consider the restrictions.</p>
<p><strong>What Public Investment Should Deliver</strong></p>
<p>As I examined in an earlier analysis of Central Asia’s <a href="https://timesca.com/central-asia-electricity-demand-summer-reckoning/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">summer electricity demand</a>, existing energy systems already need substantial modernization. Governments offering preferential power to computing companies must account for the cost to those systems and their other users. They should publish the full infrastructure costs and make local access commitments measurable, including prices and capacity available to domestic businesses.</p>
<p>A domestic company can export its own AI software using rented computing capacity. Public support should give such businesses room to grow, alongside the foreign operators receiving infrastructure and electricity incentives. Hosting international computing infrastructure can be a profitable business. Building a national AI industry is a different objective.</p>

					<description><![CDATA[Tajikistan is preparing to receive Vladimir Putin days after the European Union sanctioned Unistream, a Russian bank used by Tajik]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Tajikistan is preparing to receive Vladimir Putin days after the European Union sanctioned Unistream, a Russian bank used by Tajik migrants to send money home.</p>
<p>Putin’s November 19–20 visit, at President Emomali Rahmon’s invitation, comes after the EU sanctioned Unistream on November 16. Tajikistan is the world’s most remittance-dependent economy, with personal remittances received equivalent to <a href="https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/BX.TRF.PWKR.DT.GD.ZS?locations=TJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">57.7% of GDP</a> in 2025, according to the World Bank. In a country of 10.8 million, more than <a href="https://www.azattyqasia.org/a/v-tadzhikistane-zayavili-o-sokraschenii-trudovoy-migratsii-za-rubezh-no-ne-raskryvayut-tsifry/33807412.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">702,000 people</a> left to work abroad in 2025, most heading to Russia, according to official figures from Dushanbe.</p>
<p>Putin’s visit coincides with a regional tour by U.S. Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau, including talks in Dushanbe. Such contacts could bring new investment, but replacing the income Tajik workers earn in Russia would require employment opportunities on a far greater scale.</p>
<p>Putin’s visit follows a meeting between the two presidents in Moscow earlier this month. Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said they had <a href="https://www.interfax.ru/amp/1129367" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">“an interest in discussing in greater detail”</a> many aspects of Russian-Tajik relations. The Kremlin said the talks would cover their “strategic partnership and alliance,” alongside regional and international issues.</p>
<p><strong>A Transfer Channel Under Sanctions</strong></p>
<p>The EU’s <a href="https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX%3A32026R2574" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">official designation</a> named Unistream among the Russian financial institutions added to its restrictions. The Council says the bank offers loan-repayment grace periods to Russian military personnel, including those fighting in Ukraine. It cites this support and the banking sector’s contribution to Russian government revenue as grounds for the listing.</p>
<p>Unistream <a href="https://www.rbc.ru/politics/18/11/2026/6afd4e311e7bc5a20926e3c8" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">told <em>RBC</em> on November 18</a> that it does not provide personal loans or repayment holidays, disputing the EU’s explanation. The bank said it expected no significant operational changes because it holds no European assets and settles transfers in rubles and CIS currencies.</p>
<p>The measures freeze assets within EU jurisdiction and prohibit making funds or economic resources available to the listed entities. They form part of a wider package covering 12 individuals and 101 entities, including nearly 50 banks and financial institutions.</p>
<p>Unistream remains listed in the National Bank of Tajikistan’s published <a href="https://nbt.tj/files/payment_system/fehrist/%D0%A4%D0%B5%D1%85%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%82%D0%B8%20%D0%9E%D0%9D%D0%9F%2008.11.2024%20ru%20.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">register of payment operators</a>. Its recorded license runs until February 22, 2027. As of November 17, <em>Asia-Plus</em> <a href="https://asiaplus.news/en/2026/11/17/eu-imposes-sanctions-on-unistream-which-operates-in-tajikistan-what-will-change/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">reported no announced suspension</a> of the service in Tajikistan following the EU decision.</p>
<p>However, there is a precedent for sanctions changing how customers use the system. The U.S. Treasury <a href="https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1636" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">designated Unistream</a> in July 2023. The following month, the company stopped dollar and euro transfers between Russia and Tajikistan, while ruble transfers continued.</p>
<p>Other transfer channels have also faced disruption. In July 2026, Uzbekistan’s Aloqabank <a href="https://aloqabank.uz/en/press_center/adverts/dear-aloqabank-customers123442123/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">suspended Zolotaya Korona transfers</a> after the EU sanctioned the system’s operator and settlement center.</p>
<p><strong>Russian Earnings Support the Economy</strong></p>
<p>The World Bank’s <a href="https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/country/tajikistan" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">latest country assessment</a> says remittances support household consumption and finance imports in an economy with a limited domestic production base. It attributes much of Tajikistan’s 8.2% growth in the first half of 2026 to those inflows. The World Bank expects a weaker Russian economy and tighter migration rules to reduce the money Tajik workers send home, slowing Tajikistan’s economic growth over the medium term.</p>
<p>Access to employment in Russia is already a source of uncertainty. In July, Tajik Deputy Labor Minister Sharif Norzoda said approximately <a href="https://timesca.com/nearly-160000-citizens-of-tajikistan-listed-in-russias-controlled-persons-register/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">160,000 Tajik citizens</a>, including about 25,000 minors, appeared on Russia’s register of controlled persons. The register covers foreigners without legal grounds to remain in the country.</p>
<p>Around <a href="https://timesca.com/russia-migrant-school-fees-central-asia/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">9,000 Tajik children</a> returned from Russia in 2025 after difficulties continuing their education there, Tajikistan’s education minister said. Russia now requires foreign children entering state schools to prove legal residence and pass a Russian-language test. Enrollment among children from Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, and Kyrgyzstan fell by 27% in the 2025–26 school year.</p>
<p>The March 2024 Crocus City Hall massacre triggered a <a href="https://timesca.com/tajik-migrants-facing-xenophobia-in-russia-after-moscow-terrorist-attack/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">wave of attacks and threats</a> against Tajik migrants. In Kaluga, attackers beat three Tajik citizens, leaving one hospitalized. Employees at a barbershop where a suspect had previously worked received threatening calls and messages.</p>
<p>Tajik Foreign Minister Sirojiddin Mukhriddin criticized the response in April 2024, saying <a href="https://www.rferl.org/a/tajikistan-terror-crocus-russia-migrants-travel/32923733.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">“an ill-conceived information campaign”</a> in Russia was creating “a negative perception” of Tajiks.</p>
<p>Human Rights Watch has <a href="https://www.hrw.org/news/2025/03/17/russia-xenophobic-crackdown-central-asian-migrants" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">documented recruitment pressure</a> on Central Asian migrants, including arbitrary detention and threats of deportation to force them into Russia’s military. In October, Tajik prisoners in Volgograd <a href="https://timesca.com/tajik-prisoners-in-russia-say-they-face-pressure-to-fight-in-ukraine/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">appealed for transfer home</a>, saying they were being pressured to fight in Ukraine. “We want to go home. We are ready to serve our sentences there,” one said. The regional prison authorities denied coercing them.</p>
<p><strong>Finding Work Elsewhere</strong></p>
<p>Tajikistan’s <a href="https://timesca.com/tajikistan-migration-law-overseas-recruitment/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">new migration law</a>, signed on October 21, is an attempt to improve recruitment abroad and assistance for returning workers. It provides ratings of foreign employers based on wages and working conditions, alongside channels for migrant complaints.</p>
<p>The authorities are also pursuing employment opportunities beyond Russia, including in the Gulf states, South Korea, Japan, and the UK, but organized recruitment remains heavily concentrated in Russia. Of 35,747 Tajik citizens placed abroad through licensed organizations in 2025, more than 31,000 went there.</p>
<p>The World Bank identifies weak private-sector development as an obstacle to creating jobs at home. Its <a href="https://www.worldbank.org/en/results/2026/06/12/tajikistan-fostering-economic-transformation-and-jobs-creation" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">June assessment</a> said a quarter of the labor force leaves to work abroad because there are too few jobs at home. Much private investment goes into mining, which requires substantial capital without necessarily employing large numbers of workers.</p>
<p>In July, the World Bank approved a <a href="https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/07/01/tajikistan-accelerates-economic-reforms-with-world-bank-support" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">$100 million grant</a> to support economic reforms and employment. Measures include improving air and digital connectivity and making it easier for foreign investors to operate.</p>
<p>Rahmon <a href="https://khovar.tj/rus/2026/11/prezident-respubliki-tadzhikistan-emomali-rahmon-prinyal-zamestitelya-gossekretarya-ssha-kristofera-landau/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">met U.S. Deputy Secretary of State Landau in Dushanbe on November 18</a>, with long-term mining cooperation among the main subjects. They also discussed cooperation with American technology companies and coordination on security.</p>

The Kyrgyz musicians, now based in New York, recently brought their <i>Sounds of Kyrgyzstan</i> program to OPERA America’s National Opera Center in Manhattan as part of the <a href="https://www.kyrgyzamericanfoundation.org/eurasiafestival" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">Eurasia Festival</a>. Salimbaeva-Greenberg also played two <a href="https://timesca.com/rejuvenating-kyrgyzstans-traditional-performing-arts-raiza-syrdybaeva-director-ustatshakirt-ensemble/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">traditional Kyrgyz instruments</a>: the komuz, a three-stringed lute, and the kyl kyyak, a two-stringed older relative of the violin.</p>

<p>Introductions between pieces connected the sounds to experiences and traditions familiar in Kyrgyzstan, giving listeners a referential path into the music. The celebrated Kyrgyz composer Kalyi Moldobasanov, who co-wrote the music for the national anthem of Kyrgyzstan, featured prominently. Introducing Russian composer Vladimir Fere’s <i>Dance</i>, Sydykov argued that Soviet-era compositions deserved consideration on their artistic merits, rather than being reduced to their political context. “Music is music,” he said.</p>
<div style="width: 1920px;" class="wp-video"><video class="wp-video-shortcode" id="video-60916-1" width="1920" height="1080" preload="metadata" controls="controls"><source type="video/mp4" src="https://timesca.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/20261023_192344.mp4?_=1" /><a href="https://timesca.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/20261023_192344.mp4" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">https://timesca.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/20261023_192344.mp4</a></video></div>
<p>For Salimbaeva-Greenberg, learning the komuz had been a response to homesickness. During a visit to Kyrgyzstan, the cellist became a beginner again, sitting beside a teacher and copying hand movements. Strumming hand gestures with names such as “dolphin” and “figure eight” became part of her performance of the traditional Kyrgyz folk tune <i>Koiron Kuu</i> (<i>The Boastful Piece</i>). “I absolutely love this instrument,” she told the audience.</p>
<p>Introducing Kyrgyzstan to audiences abroad is a role both musicians know from childhood. In the early years of the country’s independence, they performed before heads of state and royalty on visits abroad with President Askar Akayev. Both participated in New Names, the Presidential Fund for Gifted Youth. Tours and performances in Russia and Türkiye followed. Sydykov credited the Meerim Foundation, established by first lady Mairam Akayeva, with supporting their group.</p>
<p>Onstage in New York, he laughed as he compared their travels aboard the presidential aircraft to flying on “Air Force One.” Kyrgyzstan gained independence in 1991, but few people they met abroad knew much about the country. For Sydykov, those journeys instilled “a sense of responsibility as cultural ambassadors for a young Central Asian republic with an ancient and remarkable heritage.”</p>
<p>Their education eventually took them in different directions. Salimbaeva-Greenberg studied at the Eastman School of Music in Rochester, New York. Sydykov trained at the Moscow State Tchaikovsky Conservatory before continuing at the <a href="https://www.azasydykov.com/artistry" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">Mannes School of Music</a> in Manhattan. They reconnected in New York City in 2011 and began performing together again.</p>
<p>“Today, as musicians based in the United States, we see this work as a continuation of the mission we embraced in childhood,” he said of their collaboration.</p>
<p>Sydykov has sought to give younger Kyrgyz musicians opportunities of their own through the <a href="https://www.kyrgyzamericanfoundation.org/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">Kyrgyz American Foundation</a>, which he co-founded in 2016 to promote musical and academic exchanges. The foundation has brought musicians selected through national competitions to perform in the U.S. He and Salimbaeva-Greenberg continue to visit Kyrgyzstan and maintain relationships with its musical community.</p>
<p>“We want this music to be a booster of pride,” Sydykov told <i>The Times of Central Asia</i>. “I think it&#8217;s very important that self-esteem is established. So we&#8217;re rebuilding these cultural ties. We want music to be this transnational language and bridge, and I think people like Kyrgyz and other Central Asians have a very ancient culture and presence in history. Our ancestors played a big role in the formation of this transnational economy,” he said, alluding to the expanding networks of the Silk Road. “Maintaining these connections is important to us because cultural exchange must be an ongoing relationship, not simply an occasional performance.”</p>
<p>The pair plan to revive their Eurasia Project, an ensemble effort that would introduce overlooked composers to listeners in Asia as well as the West. Sydykov’s commitment to Central Asian music is accompanied by a deep, almost anthropological curiosity about history and the evolution of cultures. He is fascinated by the movement of people and ideas across Eurasia and views historic exchanges along the Silk Road as a reason for musicians to work across national and linguistic boundaries today. Discussing colonialism and the influence of neighboring empires, he spoke of wanting people from smaller countries to feel confidence in their own musical inheritance. International recognition, in his view, can change how Central Asian audiences and diaspora communities see their cultures.</p>
<p>Sydykov finds encouragement in the response of fellow musicians in cities such as London and Paris. When he plays music for them that has its roots in Central Asia, they feel connected and want to perform it. “You know, there is no inferiority to this music.” The obstacle, he believes, is that many musicians have simply never heard it.</p>
<p>Sydykov and Salimbaeva-Greenberg share an infectious ambition to bring composers familiar in Central Asia into other musicians’ regular programs. Through those performances, Sydykov says, “they will have a chance to be known on the world stage.”</p>

<p>Awais Leghari, Pakistan’s energy minister, suggested the idea to Annageldi Saparov, his Turkmen counterpart, during a meeting in Islamabad on Monday.</p>

<p>Pakistan, which has suffered severe electricity shortages, high costs and other problems over the years, believes the two power transmission projects with Central Asia can help to meet its needs and develop its economy. <p>Saparov, the Turkmen energy minister, told Leghari that Turkmenistan would soon share the required data for the TAP power transmission project and that Ashgabat had completed laying the transmission line up to the Afghan border, according to the Pakistani statement.</p>
<p>Rashid Meredov, Turkmenistan’s foreign minister, was leading his country’s delegation in Islamabad for discussions with other countries on Eurasian economic issues. 					<description><![CDATA[Kazakhstan’s Mother’s Home project, which helps women facing difficult circumstances stay with their children, now has an international network of]]></description>
										<content:encoded><![CDATA[<p>Kazakhstan’s Mother’s Home project, which helps women facing difficult circumstances stay with their children, now has an international network of 14 centers in Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Ukraine, Türkiye, and Malaysia. The foundation says its international work has helped approximately 500 children remain with their mothers since 2023.</p>
<p>Across Central Asia, the United Nations Children’s Fund has called for greater investment in <a href="https://timesca.com/unicef-central-asia-must-work-to-keep-children-out-of-alternative-care/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">family support services</a> to prevent children from entering institutional care unnecessarily.</p>
<p><i>The Times of Central Asia</i> spoke with Timur Tuleuzhanov, chief executive of the privately funded <a href="https://dom-mamy.kz/ru/mezhdunarodnyy-fond" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">Mother’s Home International Foundation</a>, about how the project is being adapted in other countries and how its centers are financed. He also discussed possible expansion into Azerbaijan and Tajikistan.</p>
<p data-pm-slice="1 1 []"><strong>TCA: </strong>The Mother’s Home project began in Kazakhstan and now operates internationally. How did it begin, and what results have you seen as it has expanded abroad?</p>
<p><strong>Timur Tuleuzhanov:</strong> Mother’s Home has been operating in Kazakhstan for 13 years. It was established in 2013 by Kazakh entrepreneurs who wanted to prevent children with living parents from entering institutional care. Not every child in an institution is an orphan. Some have mothers who want to raise them but lack housing, an income, or support from relatives. In those circumstances, a mother may feel she has no choice but to give up her child.</p>
<p>Mother’s Home centers were created to help women in these circumstances. They provide temporary housing, professional support, and a chance to rebuild their lives while staying with their children.</p>
<p>Over the years, more than 10,000 children in Kazakhstan have been able to stay with their mothers thanks to the project. This experience showed us that many cases of children entering institutional care can be prevented if families receive support at the right time. Eventually, we began considering expanding Mother’s Home beyond Kazakhstan. Women in other countries face similar circumstances, and we wanted them to have access to the same kind of assistance.</p>
<p>That led to the establishment of the Mother’s Home International Foundation in 2023. Ukraine became the first country where the international project began operating that same year. Uzbekistan joined the initiative in 2024, followed by Kyrgyzstan and Malaysia in 2025. The first center in Turkey opened in 2026.</p>
<p>Today, the international network has 14 centers in five countries. Nine of those centers are in Uzbekistan, where <a href="https://podrobno.uz/cat/obchestvo/v-termeze-otkrylsya-novyy-tsentr-pomoshchi-zhenshchinam-s-detmi-okazavshimsya-v-trudnoy-situatsii/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">Ona Uyi</a> operates in Tashkent and several other regions.</p>
<p>But I consider the lives of the children, rather than the number of centers or countries, to be our most important achievement. Thanks to the international project, approximately 500 children who might otherwise have ended up in institutional care are now growing up with their mothers.</p>
<p><strong>TCA: </strong>Could you describe a specific case in which Mother’s Home helped keep a family together? What happened to the woman and her children afterward?</p>
<p><strong>Tuleuzhanov:</strong> In Uzbekistan, we support a woman named Zarnigor, a mother of three. Several years ago, she traveled to Russia in search of work. Her children were born there, but over time she encountered problems with her documents. During an inspection, Zarnigor was detained and placed in a temporary detention center for foreign nationals, while her children were sent to a social care institution. Her youngest daughter was just one week old at the time. They spent almost a year apart.</p>
<p>With the involvement of diplomats and human rights advocates, the family was reunited and returned to Uzbekistan. In April 2026, Zarnigor and her children entered our Ona Uyi center in Tashkent. She had nowhere to live and no job, but what concerned our staff most was her emotional state. She appeared completely lost, with an empty look in her eyes. After such a long separation from her children, returning to everyday life was not easy.</p>
<p>We therefore paid particular attention to psychological support. Over several months, Zarnigor attended individual counseling sessions. She gradually recovered emotionally, became more confident, and began taking a more active role in planning her family’s future. At the same time, our specialists provided the family with housing and food and helped restore their documents.</p>
<p>Four months later, Zarnigor moved with her children to our Ona Uyi center in Termez. Her life has changed considerably since then. She is now employed, and all three children attend kindergarten. We are currently helping her secure permanent housing so that she can eventually live independently with her children.</p>
<p>But Zarnigor’s story is just one example of our work. At Mother’s Home, women receive more than temporary housing. Some of the women we support have obtained government-provided apartments with assistance from our lawyers. Others have continued their education, enrolled in universities, learned new professions, and found employment. While the mothers study or work, their children attend kindergarten or remain under the supervision of center staff.</p>
<p>Our coordinators play a major role in the lives of the women we support. They are there every day, understand each family’s circumstances, help resolve everyday problems, and support women through difficult periods. For many who have spent a long time without support from relatives, our coordinators become almost like second mothers.</p>
<p><strong>TCA: </strong>A new Ona Uyi center opened in Termez in July. How does your partnership with Uzbekistan’s National Agency for Social Protection work, and how have you adapted the Kazakh model to local conditions?</p>
<p data-pm-slice="1 1 []"><strong>Tuleuzhanov:</strong> We use the same model in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, but there are certain differences in how women enter our centers and how we work with government agencies.</p>
<p>In Kazakhstan, most women contact Mother’s Home directly. We also receive referrals from maternity hospitals and outpatient clinics for pregnant women and young mothers who find themselves in difficult circumstances.</p>
<p>In Uzbekistan, we work closely with the National Agency for Social Protection. Women can contact Ona Uyi directly, but before admission, their circumstances are assessed through the Inson social service centers. Specialists evaluate their situation and, if they confirm that assistance is needed, refer them to our centers. This cooperation allows us to combine the resources of the foundation and the state social protection system to address the needs of individual families.</p>
<p>At the same time, we do not aim to replicate the Kazakh experience in every detail. Each country has its own legislation, social protection system, and approach to working with families. We preserve the project’s core principles, standards, and methodology while adapting its operations to local conditions.</p>
<p><strong>TCA: </strong>How are the centers financed? What roles do the foundation, entrepreneurs, and government agencies play?</p>
<p><strong>Tuleuzhanov:</strong> A distinctive feature of our model is that Mother’s Home centers are funded entirely by philanthropists. These are entrepreneurs who commit to covering the centers’ costs, including housing and food for women and children, staff salaries, and day-to-day expenses. We do not receive government funding.</p>
<p>We have maintained this model as we have expanded internationally. In countries where new centers open, we attract local entrepreneurs who are prepared to do more than make a one-time charitable donation. They commit to supporting the project on an ongoing basis. Long-term support is particularly important to us because a center’s operations should not depend on occasional donations. It must be able to operate consistently, accept new residents, and provide continuing assistance to families.</p>
<p>We also separate financial and operational responsibilities. Philanthropists provide funding, while the foundation is responsible for managing the centers, coordinating specialists, and supporting the families in our care. Government and local partners help address social, legal, and other issues faced by the women we support. This division of responsibilities allows us to expand the project while maintaining its stability and the quality of our work.</p>
<p><strong>TCA: </strong>Which countries does Mother’s Home plan to enter in the coming years? How could the experience of expanding abroad help strengthen cooperation between countries?</p>
<p><strong>Tuleuzhanov:</strong> We plan to continue expanding the international Mother’s Home network. Azerbaijan and Tajikistan are currently among the countries we are considering. At the same time, we will continue developing the project in countries where we already operate. In Uzbekistan, for example, we plan to open additional centers so that women in every region of the country can access assistance.</p>
<p>Over three years of international operations, we have gained experience adapting the Kazakh model to conditions in different countries. This creates opportunities for regional cooperation, including sharing practical experience, training local government specialists, and developing partnerships among entrepreneurs willing to support social initiatives.</p>
<p>Ultimately, our goal is not simply to expand Mother’s Home’s geographical reach but to ensure that as few children as possible enter residential institutions. That is why our project exists: to prevent children from being separated from their families and help them grow up with their own parents.</p>

<p>Acwa estimates that once all its energy projects in Karakalpakstan are complete, they will have a combined generating capacity of about 2.3 gigawatts and produce around 8.77 billion kilowatt-hours a year. That would cover the electricity needs of about 2.4 million households. The projects could also save around 2.1 billion cubic meters of natural gas and avoid nearly 5 million metric tons of carbon dioxide emissions each year.</p>
<p>The company expects Kungrad to <a href="https://timesca.com/karakalpakstan-investment-new-projects/" data-wpel-link="internal" class="int-link">create about 4,000 jobs</a> at peak construction. It estimates that about $750 million worth of supplies and project work could be sourced in Uzbekistan, including local manufacturing.</p>
<p>In its announcement, Uzbekistan’s ecology committee did not say when the Kungrad review would be completed or how many credits the project could generate if approved. Nor did it provide an estimate of potential revenue from carbon credit sales.</p>

<p>The plans were outlined at a November 13 <a href="https://president.uz/en/lists/view/9857" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">meeting on the draft state budget</a> and economic forecasts for 2027. <p>Authorities expect Uzbekistan&#8217;s economy to grow by at least 8% in 2026, with gross domestic product (GDP) exceeding $200 billion. <p><strong>Focus on International Companies</strong></p>
<p>During the first nine months of 2026, Uzbekistan used $44.1 billion in <a href="https://t.me/MIIT_Uz/20651" target="_blank" data-wpel-link="external" rel="follow external noopener noreferrer" class="ext-link">foreign investment and loans</a>, according to the Ministry of Investment, Industry and Trade. This exceeded the target set for the period by 23.9%. A total of 106 strategic projects were launched under the state investment program.</p>

The Trade Promotion Fund provided approximately $15 million in financial support to 1,232 business owners. According to the ministry, the assistance supported $2 billion in exports.</p>
<p>In 2027, authorities hope to attract two or three major international investors to each priority industry. <p>The plans also extend to newer industries, including semiconductors, robotics, biotechnology, and the manufacture of electric vehicle batteries and high-tech components.</p>

<p>Accession to the World Trade Organization is another priority. <p><strong>A $38.6 Billion Budget</strong></p>

These allocations represent increases of 14 trillion soms for education and 12 trillion soms for healthcare compared with 2026.</p>

<p>The government plans to maintain the main tax rates in 2027. It expects to raise additional revenue by broadening the tax base and reducing the shadow economy. Improvements to tax and customs administration are also planned.</p>
<p>Officials also plan to test a digital system for tracking public spending on construction projects. The government wants to attract more private investment into banks and use public-private partnerships to finance infrastructure.</p>
<p>The draft budget will first be reviewed by local kengashes, or representative councils, before being submitted to the Legislative Chamber of the Oliy Majlis, Uzbekistan&#8217;s parliament.</p>

<p>Minister of Industry and Construction Yersayin Nagaspayev <a href="https://www.gov.kz/org/mps/press/news/details/1330826?lang=ru" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">presented</a> the figures at a government meeting on November 17.</p>
<p>According to ministry <a href="https://www.inform.kz/ru/200-novih-promishlennih-proektov-nasummu-svishe-17trln-tenge-zapustyat-vkazahsta-d3b94a6e" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">estimates</a>, once all 200 projects reach full capacity, they could produce $3.4 billion worth of goods annually, with approximately one-third intended for export. The projects are expected to create more than 17,000 jobs, including 5,500 in rural areas.</p>
<p>Kazakhstan&#8217;s manufacturing sector <a href="https://stat.gov.kz/ru/industries/business-statistics/stat-industrial-production/publications/354936/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">grew</a> by 9.3% in January–October compared with the same period last year, while overall industrial output increased by 3.5%. Mining output fell by 3.4% over the same period.</p>
<p>Nagaspayev said the new projects would allow Kazakhstan to manufacture products not previously made domestically, including steel rims for railway wheels, gears, industrial presses, drip irrigation systems, and aluminum coffee capsules.</p>
<p>Separate, longer-term investments include two major copper smelters expected to begin production in 2030. Kazakhstan produces approximately 3.4 million metric tons of copper concentrate annually but <a href="https://www.zakon.kz/amp/obshestvo/6538898-pravitelstvo-namereno-narastit-promyshlennuyu-moshch-kazakhstana.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">processes</a> only 59% of that volume domestically. By 2030, the government aims to increase concentrate production to 5.1 million metric tons, with projects at the Koksay and Aidarly deposits expected to account for the additional 1.7 million metric tons.</p>
<p>To expand domestic processing, a copper smelter is being built in Aktogay in the Abai region. The plant will have an annual capacity of 300,000 metric tons of refined copper, with initial production expected in 2030. Construction officially <a href="https://www.akorda.kz/ru/glava-gosudarstva-v-rezhime-telemosta-dal-start-stroitelstvu-aktogayskogo-medeplavilnogo-zavoda-2093914" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">began</a> on October 20. The project, valued at approximately $1.8 billion, is being developed by KAZ Minerals with a Chinese engineering partner.</p>
<p>Another facility with the same capacity is <a href="https://primeminister.kz/ru/news/po-porucheniyu-prezidenta-v-balhashe-postroyat-novyy-medeplavilnyy-zavod-s-sozdaniem-1200-postoyannyh-rabochih-mest-31712" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">planned</a> for Balkhash in the Karaganda region. Construction is scheduled to begin in 2027, with production expected in 2030. The plant is expected to create 1,200 jobs.</p>
<p>The ministry aims to increase annual copper output from 471,000 metric tons to 1.1 million metric tons by 2030 and more than double production of finished copper products to 40,000 metric tons. Even if these targets are achieved, most of the copper industry&#8217;s output will remain metal rather than finished goods.</p>
<p>Kazakhstan is also aiming to increase the share of locally manufactured components in its automotive industry. The country already produces batteries, tires, seats, and multimedia systems. In 2026, manufacturers began producing heating and climate control systems, floor coverings, and engine blocks for trucks. Steel supplied by Temirtau-based Qarmet is used to stamp body panels for buses.</p>
<p>A plant capable of producing up to 60,000 automotive components annually is <a href="https://24.kz/ru/news/economyc/806470-klasternyj-podkhod-vnedryayut-v-obrabatyvayushchej-otrasli-kazakhstana" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">expected</a> to open in Kostanay by the end of the year. Production of automotive wiring harnesses, brake lines, and fuel lines is also being prepared in Almaty.</p>
<p>Heavy-duty vehicles are another area of development. Kazakhstan already assembles HOWO and Scania trucks, while Volvo assembly operations are expected to begin in Almaty before the end of 2026. Prime Minister Olzhas Bektenov has also reported talks with Mercedes-Benz about possible production in Kazakhstan.</p>
<p>In Saran, in the Karaganda region, the $54 million Saran Machinery project is being developed. The plant is <a href="https://www.spk-saryarka.kz/info/articles/metallurgiya/proizvodstvo-gruzovykh-avtomobiley-marki-howo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener follow external noreferrer" data-wpel-link="external" class="ext-link">designed</a> to produce up to 10,000 HOWO trucks annually. Initially, production will be limited to assembling cabs and chassis, with plans to introduce welding, painting, and the domestic manufacture of certain components at later stages. The project&#8217;s original timetable called for it to begin operating in the second quarter of 2026, but construction was still nearing completion in November.</p>
<p>The next step is to make more of those components in Kazakhstan. Several of the announced vehicle projects still depend on imported parts.</p>
